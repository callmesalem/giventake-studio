# 05 — Pricing, positioning, and go-to-market

Strategy, stated directly. You asked what someone starting this business would
pay attention to — this is that answer, including the parts that argue against
choices already made on the site.

---

## 1. Drop the $500 tier

The Essentials tier at **$500 – $2.5K** is the change I'd argue hardest against.

**Why it's a mistake:**

- **It consumes the scarcest thing you have.** A $500 project needs a sales call,
  a scope document, a contract, a kickoff, revisions, a handoff, and 14 days of
  support. The overhead is nearly identical to a $10,000 project. Two $500 jobs
  can eat a week you needed for a $15,000 one.
- **It anchors your brand at the bottom.** Price is the strongest quality signal a
  buyer has when they can't evaluate the work. A visitor who sees "$500" reads the
  whole studio as a $500 studio, including the Growth tier.
- **It selects for your worst clients.** The correlation between smallest budget
  and highest scope-dispute risk is not subtle. Clients who negotiate hardest over
  $500 are the ones who dispute the invoice.
- **It doesn't lead anywhere.** The theory is that small jobs become big ones.
  Occasionally true. Mostly, a client with a $500 budget has a $500 problem.

**What to do instead:** set a **$2,500 engagement minimum** and let Starter be the
entry point.

**If you want a low-commitment on-ramp** — and there's a real argument for one,
since a new studio needs to reduce the risk of hiring an unproven vendor — make it
a **paid discovery sprint**:

- $750–$1,500, fixed, one to two weeks
- Deliverable: a written scope, technical approach, timeline, and fixed quote
- **Fee credited against the project** if they proceed
- They keep the document either way

This is strictly better than a $500 build. It qualifies the client, gets you paid
for the work of writing a proposal (which you're doing anyway, for free, right
now), positions you as a professional rather than a bargain, and produces exactly
the written scope that prevents disputes later.

**Your contact form's budget dropdown originally started at $2,500** — the form
was built around the right instinct before the $500 tier was added. That's worth
noticing.

## 2. "AI-native" is a commoditizing position

The current positioning is "AI-native development studio" / "agentic speed with
human checkpoints." It's well-executed, and the "Quality & Guardrails" section is
a genuinely good answer to the obvious objection.

**But it has a shelf life.** Within a year every agency will claim it, the claim
is unfalsifiable from the buyer's side, and the buyer's real question isn't "do
you use AI" — it's "will this work, and will you still be here in a year."
AI-native answers neither. Worse, leading with your method invites the response:
_if AI is doing the work, why am I paying you?_ Your FAQ answers this well, but
you shouldn't have to answer it on every call.

**The durable moat is domain specialization.** A generalist who is fast competes
on price with every other fast generalist. A specialist who understands the
customer's business competes on nobody else's terms.

## 3. Your unfair advantage is in your own email address

You run an insurance claims business.

That is worth more than any technical differentiator on the site. It means:

- **Real domain knowledge.** You know how claims and restoration businesses
  actually operate — the intake, the documentation burden, the carrier
  back-and-forth, the scheduling, where the money leaks. You cannot acquire this
  by reading. Your competitors would need months of discovery to learn what you
  already know, and they'd still get the vocabulary wrong on the first call.
- **A real network.** Adjusters, contractors, restoration firms, agencies. These
  are warm introductions, not cold outreach. For a new studio with no
  track record, warm introductions are the entire game.
- **A market that's genuinely underserved.** Claims, restoration, and trades
  businesses run on spreadsheets, phone tag, paper, and software built in 2009.
  The work is repetitive, high-volume, and document-heavy — which is precisely
  where AI automation delivers real, measurable value rather than novelty.
- **Instant credibility.** "I run a claims business and I build software for
  claims businesses" is a complete answer to "why should I trust you," from a
  studio that otherwise has no track record. It converts your biggest weakness
  into your positioning.

**Recommended position:**

> Software and AI automation for claims, restoration, and trades businesses —
> built by someone who runs one.

**And it solves the case-studies problem.** Your own operation is a legitimate
first reference build. Automate your own intake, your own document handling, your
own scheduling — then write it up with real numbers you can substantiate, because
you measured them yourself. That's a real case study, published honestly, in
weeks rather than after landing a stranger.

**This doesn't mean turning away other work.** Keep the general site and take the
general projects. But lead with the vertical, and let the general capability be
what you mention second. Specialists get referred; generalists get shopped.

## 4. Retainers are the business

Project revenue is lumpy, and every month restarts at zero. You will spend a
predictable share of every month selling instead of building, and the months you
build hardest are the months the pipeline dies.

The **Dedicated** tier is the one to actually sell:

- Predictable revenue that survives a slow month
- Compounding client knowledge — month six is far more productive than month one
- No repeated sales cycle
- It's what "your on-demand development team" actually means; the other tiers
  are projects

**Structure it as:** a monthly fee for a defined capacity, a shared roadmap the
client can reprioritise monthly, weekly demos, 30-day cancellation. That's close
to what's on the site already. The change is which tier you push in
conversation — sell projects as the on-ramp to a retainer, not as the product.

**VERIFY (CPA):** how the retainer is invoiced has direct sales-tax consequences.
See [02-ohio-tax.md](./02-ohio-tax.md) §1 — the bundled retainer is the worst
case for apportionment, so structure the invoice before you sell the first one.

## 5. Rates and the honesty positioning

The founder note says _"Rates reflect a team building its own track record, not
agency overhead."_ That's an honest and effective frame — but it has an
expiration date, and you should plan for it deliberately.

- **Discount for the first three clients explicitly and temporarily.** Say the
  number is an introductory rate and what it becomes afterward. A discount framed
  as permanent is a permanent discount.
- **Trade the discount for something.** A written testimonial, permission to
  publish a case study with real metrics, a referral introduction. The reference
  is worth more than the margin at this stage — it is the asset you don't have.
- **Raise rates after the third client.** Do it on schedule, not when you feel
  ready. You will never feel ready.

## 6. Getting to real case studies

This is the highest-leverage thing on the list, because it retires the problem
that forced the site rewrite.

1. **Build for yourself first.** Your claims business is a real client with real
   problems and real numbers. Measure before and after honestly.
2. **First three clients from your existing network.** Not cold outreach, not
   ads. Warm introductions convert at a rate cold traffic cannot approach, and a
   studio with no track record cannot afford a low conversion rate.
3. **Ask for permission to publish, in the contract.** Include a clause covering
   use of the client's name and results as a reference. Asking after delivery
   gets a "let me check with legal" that never resolves.
4. **Measure the baseline before you start.** You cannot claim "reduced admin
   time by 60%" without knowing what it was. Ten minutes of measurement at
   kickoff is what makes a publishable claim possible eight weeks later.
5. **Get the testimonial at the moment of delivery**, when they're happiest —
   not three months later when the glow has faded.

Every fabricated metric that came off the site can go back on, honestly, once
there's a real project behind it. That's the goal.

## 7. What to watch in the first year

The numbers that actually tell you whether this is working:

- **Effective hourly rate** — project fee ÷ hours actually spent, including sales,
  scoping, and revisions. This is the number that reveals whether small projects
  are worth it. Track it from the first project or you'll never know.
- **Utilization** — billable vs. total hours. Solo operators consistently
  overestimate this; 50–60% is realistic once sales and admin are counted.
- **Pipeline coverage** — signed and likely work for the next 60 days. The month
  you stop selling is the month two months from now with no revenue.
- **Scope creep per project** — hours beyond the SOW. If this is consistently
  high, the problem is your scoping, not your clients.
- **Cash runway** — months of personal expenses covered. This determines whether
  you can decline a bad client, which is the single most valuable ability a solo
  business can have.

**The failure mode to watch for:** taking every project that comes, staying busy,
and discovering after a year that the effective hourly rate never justified the
work. Busy is not the same as viable. Track the rate.
