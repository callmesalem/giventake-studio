# 04 — AI delivery policy

This is the document that matters most, and the one your competitors mostly
haven't written.

Every studio is about to claim "AI-native." Almost none of them can answer a
client's procurement questions about it: _whose model, retaining what, trained on
what, who owns the output, what happens when it reproduces someone else's code._
Being able to answer those questions in writing is a sales asset before it is a
compliance artefact — it converts your biggest perceived risk into evidence that
you have thought harder than the alternative.

It also has to be true. Your website makes specific public commitments —
"AI-generated code is reviewed by a human before it ships," "Nothing ships
without my manual review," "not used to train AI models." Those are
representations now. This document is what makes them substantiated rather than
aspirational.

---

## 1. The AI tool register

**Maintain [`../contracts/ai-tool-register.md`](../contracts/ai-tool-register.md)
and keep it current.** For every AI tool used in delivery, record:

- Tool and vendor
- Plan/tier (this is what determines the data terms — consumer tiers and business
  tiers differ substantially)
- **Does it train on inputs?** Yes / No / Configurable-and-configured-off
- **Data retention period**
- Whether a zero-data-retention option exists and is enabled
- Commercial use rights over output
- Whether a DPA is available and signed
- Date verified, and by whom

**Why it exists:** it substantiates the public claim on your contact form, it
answers a client security questionnaire in one attachment instead of three weeks
of email, and it forces you to actually check the tier you're on. Re-verify
quarterly — vendors change terms, and consumer-tier defaults are usually the
wrong ones.

**Rule: no client material goes into a tool that isn't on the register.**

## 2. Copyright — the trap most dev shops are walking into

**The U.S. Copyright Office's position is that purely AI-generated material is
not protectable by copyright.** Human authorship is required; output generated by
a model from a prompt, without sufficient human creative contribution, does not
get copyright protection.

This has a direct and under-appreciated contractual consequence:

> **You cannot honestly warrant that a client will own an exclusive copyright in
> AI-generated code.** There may be no copyright to own.

Standard dev-shop contracts say something like _"Developer assigns all right,
title and interest, and warrants that the Deliverables are original works."_ If a
meaningful portion of the deliverable was generated by an AI agent, that warranty
may be false — and it is a warranty the client will rely on if they ever try to
enforce rights against a third party.

**How to handle it (reflected in the MSA template):**

- **Assign everything you actually hold.** "Developer assigns all right, title and
  interest that Developer holds in the Deliverables" — this is true regardless of
  how much is AI-generated.
- **Do not warrant that exclusive copyright subsists** in AI-generated portions.
  Disclose the limitation instead of papering over it.
- **Warrant what you can:** that you haven't knowingly incorporated third-party
  material without a licence, that you've run provenance checks, and that you
  aren't aware of infringing content.
- **Say it out loud in the sales conversation.** A client who learns this from
  their own lawyer after signing reacts very differently from one who heard it
  from you before signing. In practice this is rarely a deal-breaker — for
  business software, what clients actually need is the right to use, modify, and
  resell the software, which the assignment gives them.

Your Terms page now states this publicly. Keep the MSA consistent with it.

**VERIFY (attorney):** exact warranty language. This is the highest-value hour of
legal time you will buy.

## 3. Open-source contamination

AI coding agents are trained on public code, including copyleft-licensed code,
and can reproduce it — sometimes verbatim, usually without attribution and always
without the licence header.

The risk is asymmetric and delayed: a GPL fragment inside a client's proprietary
product is a problem that surfaces during their acquisition due diligence, years
later, and it is a problem you handed them.

**Controls, per delivery:**

- [ ] Run a **licence/composition scan** before handover (ScanCode, FOSSA, Syft,
      or equivalent)
- [ ] Generate an **SBOM** and deliver it with the code — this is increasingly a
      client requirement anyway, and it makes you look like a serious vendor
- [ ] Configure AI tools to **suppress suggestions matching public code** where
      that setting exists (several major coding assistants offer it)
- [ ] Record that the scan was run and what it found, per project. The record is
      what turns "we don't know" into "we checked and here's the result"
- [ ] Flag any copyleft dependency to the client **before** delivery, not in a
      footnote after

**Contract posture:** warrant that you ran provenance checks and are not aware of
infringing material. Do not warrant that the code is free of all third-party
material — no one can honestly warrant that, with or without AI.

## 4. Client data in AI tools

Your delivery model means client material — code, schemas, sometimes real data —
passes through third-party AI services. That has to be disclosed and consented
to, not assumed.

**Requirements:**

- **Written consent to AI-assisted delivery in the MSA**, obtained before work
  starts. Retrofitting consent after a client discovers how the work was done is
  a far worse conversation, and it is the kind of thing that ends a relationship
  even when nothing went wrong.
- **Business/enterprise tiers with training disabled**, verified against the
  register.
- **A DPA** where the material includes personal data, and especially where
  EU/UK data subjects are involved.
- **Never put production personal data into an AI tool** for debugging. Use
  synthetic or anonymised data. This is the most likely way you would cause an
  actual breach, and it happens because it's convenient at 11pm.
- **Never put secrets into an AI tool** — API keys, credentials, tokens. Use
  environment variables and placeholder values.
- **Some clients will say no.** Regulated industries, or clients with their own AI
  policies, may prohibit third-party AI processing of their material. Ask early;
  finding out at signature costs you the deal, finding out at delivery costs you
  the relationship.

## 5. The human review gate — make it real

Your website commits to specific checkpoints: Plan (human-led), Code (AI agents),
Test (AI + human), Review (human checkpoint), Deploy (human-approved), and
"Nothing ships without my manual review."

These are good commitments and good marketing. They are also now representations
that a disappointed client's lawyer will quote back to you. Make them operational:

**Minimum review standard before anything ships:**

- [ ] Every AI-generated file read by a human, not skimmed
- [ ] **Auth, authorization, and payment paths reviewed line by line** — these
      are where negligence claims concentrate, and where AI output is most
      confidently wrong
- [ ] Data model and migrations reviewed by hand
- [ ] Automated tests exist for critical paths and actually pass
- [ ] Licence scan run (§3)
- [ ] No secrets committed; scan the repo before handover
- [ ] Dependency vulnerability check

**Keep a lightweight record per project.** A checklist in the repo is enough. The
point is that "we reviewed it" is demonstrable rather than asserted.

**What stays human, per your own FAQ:** architecture decisions, stack choices,
UX design, security review, client communication, final sign-off. That list is a
good one — it's the honest answer to "so what am I paying you for," and it should
stay stable, because changing it later looks like retreat.

## 6. Marketing claims about AI

The FTC has an active enforcement posture on overstated AI claims, and the
substantiation standard is the ordinary one: you must have a reasonable basis for
an objective claim **before** you make it.

**Rules for this site and every proposal:**

- No performance or outcome claims without documented evidence from real work
  (this is why the case studies were rewritten)
- No "AI-powered" framing for things that aren't
- Process claims must describe the process you actually follow
- Speed claims ("weeks, not quarters") should stay qualitative and hedged unless
  you can back a specific number with delivered projects
- **Anything a client relies on in deciding to hire you needs to be true** — the
  legal test is materiality, not literal accuracy

## 7. Incident response

Your privacy policy commits to notifying affected parties and the relevant
authority **within 72 hours** of a breach. That clock is short, and it starts
before you understand what happened.

## 8. Security questionnaire pack

Clients above a certain size will send a security questionnaire, and answering it
from scratch stalls a deal for weeks. Assemble the answers once:

- AI tool register (§1)
- Data handling and retention practices
- Access control and secrets management
- Backup and recovery
- Incident response summary (§7)
- Insurance certificate
- Subcontractor policy (currently: none)

You do not need SOC 2. You need to answer the questionnaire in two days instead
of three weeks. Revisit certification only if enterprise clients start requiring
it — and if they do, that's a good problem.
