# 08 — Marketing and client acquisition

**Constraints this plan is built for:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Positioning** | Generalist studio — "AI-native development for small business" |
| **Brand voice** | Faceless. Studio voice, no founder name, no photo, **no headcount claims** |
| **Budget** | Under $250/month for the first six months |
| **Capacity** | Solo — realistically 2–3 concurrent projects |
| **Proof** | None yet. No case studies, no reviews, no track record |

---

## Start here: the honest read

You've chosen the hardest of the available combinations, so the plan has to be
built around that rather than pretending otherwise.

- **Generalist** means competing with every agency and freelancer on earth for the
  same searches, with no domain advantage to shortcut trust.
- **Faceless** removes the cheapest trust signal a new studio has. B2B services
  buyers buy from people, and referrals attach to people.
- **Under $250/month** takes paid acquisition off the table entirely for now
  (§7 explains why in numbers).
- **No proof** means every channel that relies on credibility is running on empty
  until the first two case studies exist.

None of that is fatal. It does mean **your only real currency is time**, and the
plan has to spend it where conversion is highest rather than where reach is
widest. That is why this plan is roughly 70% direct outreach and partnerships,
30% compounding assets — and 0% advertising.

### The bridge: be a generalist company that sells specific things

The single most useful move available to you, and it costs nothing.

You've decided not to commit to a vertical. Fine — but **the reason a vertical
works isn't the industry, it's the specificity.** Specific offers convert; "we
build software" doesn't. A generalist studio can sell specific *offers* without
being locked to a specific *industry*:

> ❌ "We build websites, apps, internal tools, and automations."
> ✅ "Replace five spreadsheets with one dashboard. From $6,000, live in 4 weeks."

That single change gives you most of what a vertical would have:

- Something a referral partner can repeat accurately from memory
- A long-tail search target you can actually rank for
- A cold email that reads as relevant rather than generic
- An anchor price that pre-qualifies the buyer before the call

Build **four to six** of these. They are the backbone of every channel below.
Your Work section already describes the right four — they just need to become
real pages with prices instead of cards.

## 1. Warm network — where your first clients actually come from

**Highest conversion, zero cost, and it does not conflict with faceless.**

Being faceless in public does not mean being impersonal in private. You can have
no personal brand online and still speak to 80 people who already know you. The
"no face" decision governs marketing collateral — not conversations.

You have a genuinely useful network from the claims business and the contracting
years: contractors, adjusters, suppliers, trades, agencies, accountants. These
people run or serve small businesses drowning in manual process. That's the buyer.

### How to work it

- [ ] **Build a list of 80–100 named people.** Phone contacts, old email, LinkedIn
      connections, past customers, suppliers, family friends who run businesses
- [ ] **Message individually.** Not a blast, not a newsletter. One message at a
      time, referencing something real about them
- [ ] **Ask for referrals, not work.** "Who do you know who's still running their
      business off spreadsheets?" is far easier to answer than "do you want to
      buy software." It also removes the awkwardness of selling to a friend
- [ ] **Pace it — 10 per week.** This is a two-month campaign, not one afternoon
- [ ] **Follow up once, 10 days later.** Most replies come from the follow-up

**Realistic expectation:** 80 messages → ~25 replies → ~12 real conversations →
~4 proposals → **1–2 clients.** That is a good outcome and it costs you nothing
but time.

## 2. Referral partners — the most underrated channel for a new studio

**This is where I'd spend the most effort after the warm list.**

Some people are asked "do you know a developer?" several times a year and have no
good answer. Become their answer. It works perfectly with a faceless brand
because **the partner is the trust proxy** — their vouch replaces your missing
founder story.

### Who to approach, in priority order

1. **Accountants and bookkeepers.** They see the spreadsheet chaos before anyone
   else, they're trusted advisors, and they have no competing offering. Best
   partner category by a distance.
2. **IT / MSP shops.** They do infrastructure and support, not custom software.
   Natural complement, zero competition, and they get asked constantly.
3. **Marketing and design agencies.** They receive development requests they
   can't fulfil and currently turn away or subcontract badly.
4. **Business brokers, SBDCs, chambers of commerce.** Lower volume, but they talk
   to owners at exactly the moment they're rethinking operations.
5. **Trade associations** in the industries you already know.

### How to make it work

- [ ] **Target 20 partners, aim for 3 real relationships.** Most will go nowhere;
      that's the expected shape
- [ ] **Lead with what they get**, not what you want. "I take the custom software
      requests you can't service, and you look good for having an answer"
- [ ] **Offer a referral fee** — 10–15% of first project revenue is standard.
      **Put it in writing**, and check whether your partner's professional body
      restricts referral fees (accountants sometimes have rules here)
- [ ] **Give them a one-page leave-behind** listing your productized offers with
      prices. Partners cannot refer what they cannot describe
- [ ] **Report back on every referral.** A partner who never hears the outcome
      stops referring

## 3. Google Business Profile — free, and do it this week

The highest-value free asset available to you, and fully faceless-compatible: it
lists a business, not a person.

- [ ] Create the profile as a **service-area business** (you don't need a
      storefront, and you can hide the street address while still serving a
      radius)
- [ ] Categories: software company, website designer, business-to-business service
- [ ] List every productized offer as a service with a price
- [ ] **This is your review infrastructure.** Reviews are the trust substitute
      that matters most when there's no face on the brand — ask every client at
      delivery, when they're happiest
- [ ] Post updates occasionally; it modestly helps local ranking

**Why this matters more for you than most:** "software developer near me" and
Cleveland-area equivalents have real volume and far weaker competition than
national terms. It's the one search surface a new studio can actually win.

**Depends on:** the business address decision from
[01-structure-and-formation.md](./01-structure-and-formation.md).

## 4. Productized offer pages — the compounding asset

Build **four to six pages**, one per offer. Each needs: the problem in the
buyer's words, what they get, a real starting price, a timeline, what's excluded,
and a single call to action.

Suggested set, drawn from what the site already describes:

| Offer | Anchor |
|---|---|
| Internal dashboard replacing spreadsheets | From $6,000 · 4 weeks |
| AI lead intake and routing | From $4,500 · 3 weeks |
| Booking and payments for service businesses | From $5,000 · 3–4 weeks |
| Marketing site with a CMS | From $2,500 · 2–3 weeks |
| Paid discovery sprint | $750–$1,500, credited to the build |

*(Prices illustrative — set them against
[05-pricing-and-positioning.md](./05-pricing-and-positioning.md).)*

Each page is simultaneously an SEO target, a cold-email offer, a referral
leave-behind, and a sales tool. This is the highest-leverage build work on the
list.

**Payback is 3–9 months for the SEO component.** Start now precisely because it's
slow.

## 5. Content — narrow, useful, and brand-voiced

Faceless works fine here as long as the content is utility rather than
personality. Two pieces a month, aimed at people already deciding.

Topics that attract buyers rather than browsers:

- "What does a custom internal tool actually cost?" — everyone searches this,
  almost nobody answers honestly. Publishing real numbers is differentiating
- "Should you buy off-the-shelf software or build it?"
- "How to tell whether your business is ready to automate something"
- "What you should get at handoff from a development project"
- **Publish the AI use disclosure publicly.** It's already written
  ([`../contracts/ai-use-disclosure.md`](../contracts/ai-use-disclosure.md)), it
  answers the objection every prospect has, and almost no competitor has anything
  comparable. This is your best content asset and it already exists.

**What not to do:** generic "10 benefits of AI" posts. No differentiation, no
ranking, no leads.

## 6. Cold outreach — after the offer pages exist

Legal and workable, but only once you have something specific to offer.

- **CAN-SPAM applies:** commercial email needs a **valid physical postal address**
  and a working opt-out. This is another reason to sort the business address
  before marketing starts — ties back to
  [01-structure-and-functionality](./01-structure-and-formation.md)
- **Specific beats broad.** One offer, one recipient type, one sentence of
  evidence you understand their problem
- **Small volume, high quality.** 20 well-researched emails beat 500 generic ones,
  and they don't burn your domain reputation
- **Use a subdomain** for cold sending so deliverability problems never touch your
  main business email

## 7. Advertising — you asked directly, so: not yet

**The short answer is no, and the numbers are the reason.**

At under $250/month:

- Google Ads clicks for development services run roughly **$15–40+**, because
  you'd be bidding against agencies with real budgets and lifetime values
- $250 buys you roughly **6–15 clicks a month**
- At a realistic 2–5% click-to-enquiry rate, that's **zero to one lead a month**,
  and not a qualified one

That's not a marketing budget, it's a rounding error in a competitive auction.

**The deeper problem is sequencing.** Advertising amplifies an offer that already
converts. Yours is unproven: no case studies, no reviews, no face, no data on
what a visitor does. Paying to send strangers to that page tests nothing and
teaches you nothing — you can't tell a bad ad from a bad offer from a bad price.

**Spend the time instead.** §1 and §2 cost nothing and convert far better,
because a referral arrives pre-trusted, which is exactly what you're short of.

### When advertising becomes the right move

All four, not any one:

1. **Two real case studies** with permission to publish and measured numbers
2. **A productized offer** with a known close rate from real conversations
3. **A landing page per offer**, not a homepage
4. **Budget of $1,000+/month sustained for three months** — anything less can't
   escape the noise floor

### If you want to test earlier anyway

The only defensible small-budget experiment:

- **$10/day, one offer, one landing page**
- **Long-tail local intent only** — "job tracking software for small business
  Cleveland", never "software development"
- **Run 6 weeks minimum** before judging; less than that is noise
- Conversion tracking configured before the first dollar. Your consent system
  already handles this correctly — GA4 is consent-gated, so **be aware analytics
  only fire for visitors who accept**, which will understate traffic

## 8. What faceless costs you, and how to cover it

Being clear about the trade so you can compensate deliberately.

| What you lose | How to cover it |
|---|---|
| Founder trust and personal credibility | Reviews on Google Business Profile; insurance certificate on request; written contracts; the AI disclosure |
| **LinkedIn organic** — company pages get almost no reach; the platform rewards personal profiles | Use LinkedIn for *research and outreach*, not publishing. Outreach from a personal account isn't public branding |
| Speaking, podcasts, community visibility | Referral partnerships (§2) — someone else's face vouching for your brand |
| Warmth that makes "new studio" charming rather than risky | Radical specificity: exact prices, exact timelines, exact exclusions. Precision reads as competence |

**One thing to be realistic about:** this is *no personal brand*, not anonymity.
You'll be on every sales call. Your name is on the Ohio SOS record, and it has to
appear on contracts and invoices. Clients will know who you are — they just won't
meet you through marketing. That's a completely reasonable position; it just
isn't a hiding place, so don't build anything that depends on it being one.

## 9. The first 90 days

| Weeks | Focus |
|---|---|
| **1–2** | Site voice pass; business address and domain live; Google Business Profile created; build the 80-person list |
| **3–6** | Warm outreach, 10/week. Start the 4–6 productized offer pages |
| **5–10** | Referral partner outreach — 20 targets, 3 relationships. Leave-behind one-pager |
| **6–12** | Build the reference project. **Measure baselines before you start** so results are publishable |
| **Ongoing** | Two content pieces a month. Ask every client for a review at delivery |
| **Week 12** | Review: which activity produced *conversations*? Double down there, cut the rest |

---

## 10. Track these, or you're guessing

With no budget, time allocation is the only decision you make — so measure what
earns it.

- **Conversations started** per channel, per week
- **Proposals sent**, and close rate
- **Source of every lead** — ask on the contact form or on the call
- **Time spent per channel** vs. conversations produced. This is the one that
  tells you what to stop doing
- **Reviews collected** — the compounding trust asset that substitutes for a face

**The failure mode to watch:** spending three months on content and SEO because
it feels productive and avoids rejection, while the warm list sits untouched.
Outreach is uncomfortable and it is where your first clients are.
